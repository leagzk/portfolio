At 2,500 people, a brand isn't a charter, it's a language.

# Sanofi IT brand platform & Product Design Foundations

At Sanofi, I built **IT's brand platform** to make an invisible function legible - then handed its people the language to talk about it themselves. Positioning, territory, personality, tone of voice, and the **production system** that carried all four into the hands of people who had never opened a design tool.

Then in 2022 the company unified every internal identity, and I took the part nobody had defined: what that brand becomes on a screen. Starting in 2018 as **Digital Communication Service Owner**, running the platform end to end - brand, communication, production - then **Product Design Lead** from 2021, when Sanofi created its Consumer Experience team.

01 - The results

## A brand only counts when people use it without asking.

They did - and the proof is what they made with it, **not what I drew.**

The platform was never judged on how it looked. It was judged on whether people reached for the tools instead of improvising around them.

Adopted without being enforced - and re-adopted when the identity underneath it changed. That's the only result a brand platform can claim.

From COMEX to front-line, with no bottleneck in the middle - because the system lived in the tools they already had open, not only in the ones designers use.

02 - The problem

## IT wasn't a support function. It was a *business driver* - and nobody outside IT could see it.

That was the brief, and it wasn't a branding one. Sanofi needed its IT to weigh more across the value chain, and the obstacle was perception: everyone treated as a service desk what was actually driving business value. Its role went unexplained, its image was dated, and its communication was scattered across the whole function, every team improvising the brand.

And illegibility has a price every multinational knows by name. Teams that can't see where IT begins or ends stop waiting and build their own - shadow IT, the tax an organisation pays for a function it can't read. A brand doesn't solve that on its own. Nothing solves it without one.

A logo doesn't move that. Perception doesn't shift because the assets got prettier - it shifts when the function becomes legible, and when everyone inside it can say the same thing about what it does. **That's two problems, and solving one without the other solves nothing.** Understand first, systematize second. The move started here.

03 - The solution

## You can't templatize what nobody understands yet.

Before a single template, I did the harder work: **classifying and vulgarizing what IT actually did** - naming its place in the value chain in words a manufacturing lead or a medical rep could use, then translating it graphically so it could be *seen*, not just read.

That understanding is what a brand platform is built on, and I built the whole one: **positioning, territory, personality, tone of voice** - what IT stood for, and how it was allowed to sound. Not decoration laid over the function. The argument for it, made explicit.

And none of it was built off to the side. The platform derived from Sanofi's own brand principles and stayed answerable to them - coherent with the corporate brand, and still distinct enough to be recognized on sight inside the ecosystem.

The Sanofi brand was the constraint.**The IT world was the playground.**

Then the system that carried it: a colour architecture that scaled across entities, a visual language with its own rules of composition, and each digital entity given its own personality - matched to the business it served, and unmistakably Sanofi.

None of which is worth anything until someone can pick it up. So the production line: **shared libraries on Creative Cloud**, where every asset lived once and updated everywhere at once - social, Microsoft suite, illustration kits, newsletters typed by objective rather than by sender. **A template per intent, not per person.**

All of it built and systematized in the Adobe suite, for the people whose job is craft - then translated into the tools everyone else opens every morning. **PowerPoint included, yes sir.**

04 - The adoption

## Nobody asks for **an umbrella**. They ask for their own logo.

Until then, everyone had done what they wanted. Every entity had its own visual habits, and every new program launched wanting its own logo - because a logo is what having a program looks like. **Consolidation reads as a loss long before it reads as a gain.** That was the real obstacle, and it wasn't a design problem.

So I started where the decisions were made. Department by department across IT, I worked the leadership teams - and I didn't sell coherence, which nobody wants. I sold what coherence buys them: their people stop spending time on form and spend it on substance, one identity covers uses they'd otherwise fund one by one, and a program gets recognized because it belongs to something, not despite it.

> ITS R&D Quarterly Report

> ITS R&D Newsletter

And I anchored the brand in what those leaders were already accountable for. Their strategy became the vehicle: the identity spoke through their priorities instead of alongside them, so adopting it wasn't extra work - it was how their agenda got heard. **Change management doesn't run beside the strategy. It rides it.**

Selling it was the start.
**Staying in the room was the job.**

Each of us was the point of contact for a given department - mine were Vaccines, R&D and Medical - not as a gate, as a catalyst: training teams, surfacing what they were missing, and adjusting the system against what came back. Leadership's messages and the front-line's assets pulled from the same platform, instead of two versions of the same story.

And a brand this size has to be taught, not announced. But "come to the session" is not a strategy for a function spread across time zones - so the training ran at three speeds. **Guides on the intranet**, for the person who needs it at 9pm. **Short videos**, for what you'd rather be shown than read. And the live formats for what needs a room: **sharing sessions** where an IT team walked everyone through a subject it owned, the platform reveal - what it was, how to adopt it, how to run the kits - and strategic webinars carried by leadership in person.

Same content, three ways in. **Nobody should have to guess how to use the thing, and nobody should have to wait for a calendar invite to find out.** Contribution and continuous improvement weren't policies. **They were the only way a shared identity survives contact with fifteen roadmaps.**

Not a charter people obey.**A language people use.**

05 - The operations

## I didn't just standardize the assets. I standardized **the practice**.

The tools made people autonomous. But autonomy without a shared practice just scatters faster - so I standardized how the communication team itself worked.

Four things, one way of running each - and each one feeds the next.

**Rituals — three directions, one practice.**

Inward, a design review where my work-study students and everyone with hands in the system showed the work and had it challenged. Across the team, a standing alignment between contact points - each of us owned a business, none of us should advance blind to the others. Outward, a recurring conversation with my perimeters, where needs surfaced early enough to be planned instead of absorbed. **Craft, cohesion, demand.**

**Editorial calendar — run like a roadmap.**

Because it was one. Needs centralized from every digital entity, arbitrated together, locked for the quarter, reviewed at the next - so what shipped stayed on point instead of drifting toward whoever asked loudest. What came next stopped being a personal habit. **It became a commitment made with the people it bound.**

**Communication plans — nobody starts from a blank page.**

One standard format, structure already decided, so the time went into the content that carried value instead of the shape holding it. Same artefact in both rooms - the one we worked from, the one we brought to the IT leadership board. And it spread past my team without anyone mandating it, which meant any plan could be read by anyone. **A shared format is one nobody has to explain.**

**Campaign metrics — produced by default.**

Engagement rate, open rate, reach, on dashboards that filled themselves rather than waiting to be asked. The dashboard is the easy part. What makes numbers mean anything is the engineering under them: one collection method, one reporting frequency, one reading grid, cycle after cycle. **Standardize how you measure, or you're not comparing campaigns - you're comparing methods.**

Numbers only cover what they can see. So every quarter we ran a qualitative survey on the rest - starting with whether the sessions and the webinars had been worth the time people gave them.

That changed the job. When the signal arrives on its own, a communication team stops defending its choices and starts adjusting them - and what it learns goes back into the next ritual, and the next quarter's calendar. *Measured while it ran - not after the fact.*

And I built the autonomy into people, not just process: I mentored two work-study students and levelled up colleagues until they could run the system themselves. Self-service isn't a portal - it's the day nobody needs to ask you. **Knowledge shared is autonomy that stays** - the same conviction I'd scale into Elements' 35+ contributors years later.

The *what* is assets, templates, identity. The *how* is practices, method, measurement. Fragmentation lives in both, so I fixed both.

The ***why*** is simpler. A digital function scattered across entities was meant to end up feeling like one - not looking unified, being unified, by people who worked the same way and could recognize each other's work at a glance. A shared identity makes a function legible to the outside. **A shared practice makes it a community to itself.**

06 - The version change

## A reorg is a breaking change. I shipped a major.

Then the function changed underneath the brand. In 2020, Sanofi moved its IT from service provider to enabler and stood up its first product teams - one notch up, inside a digital transformation running across the whole company. New digital leadership came with it, and the digital identity was reset from the top. **I didn't defend mine. I versioned it.**

Positioning moved. Territory moved. **The problem underneath didn't move an inch.**

Leadership picked 01. I'd argued for 02 - but 01 was the shorter migration, and the shortest path for the people moving is the one I've defended everywhere else. Taste on one side, adoption on the other. I arbitrated, and I shipped 01 like it had been my idea.

It was still the one I'd started with: nobody outside IT could see where IT began or ended, and shadow IT kept filling the gap. But the foundations held - so this wasn't a rebuild, it was a refinement. A leaner brand structure, organized around what the function had actually become: products and service lines, not departments. V1 had made IT legible. V2 made it navigable.

What I didn't rebuild was the machine. The editorial calendar, one contact point per department, the sharing sessions, the webinars carried by leadership - all of it held, and all of it went to work on the migration. The campaigns stopped being campaigns about the brand. They became the migration path: pushing usage, walking teams off the old templates, getting people to upgrade. **Communication wasn't announcing the system. It was deploying it.**

There's no deprecation warning in PowerPoint. The old deck still opens, and it always will. You can't break the previous version - you can only make the next one worth the switch.

Years later I'd have the word for it: a major. Breaking change, migration path, adopters to move off the old version. I shipped one before I knew what to call it.

07 - The turning point

## A brand book doesn't survive contact with a screen.

The first time the ground moved, it was one function. The second time, it was the whole company. In 2022, Sanofi unified every internal identity into a single brand. The part nobody had defined was what that brand becomes once it's interactive - so I took it: **translating the new corporate identity through a digital prism**, and shipping the first concepts that proved it could hold on a product, not just on a page.

I wasn't the only one moving - and by then I'd moved too. The product teams that appeared in the last reset had turned into a function: Sanofi stood up product design in 2021 with the creation of the Consumer Experience team, and I joined it as **Product Design Lead**, carrying four years of brand and communication in with me. So when the rebrand landed, the two forces looking for each other - brand on one side, product on the other - met in a role that had lived in both. **That's the gap I've always worked in.**

Translating the brand meant looking hard at what it would land on. So I ran the discovery - a broad benchmark of the field, then an audit of what Sanofi already had. The finding was familiar: **UI kits and design systems everywhere, one per entity, none speaking to the others.** The exact fragmentation I'd spent four years removing from communication, sitting one layer deeper - in the interface itself.
