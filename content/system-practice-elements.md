At global scale a system isn't managed, it's governed.

# Elements - Sanofi global experience system

At Sanofi, I built Elements to remove the frictions between experts, reduce time to launch and enhance product experience at scale. Moving then from a design system as a product to one platform, one pipeline, brand and product and engineering pouring into the same chain.

But the external recognition that means the most to me is the category we were named in at the **global Design System Awards 2025: Governance, finishing finalist alongside HP and Tesco.** Because this was never only a craft story. It was a leadership one. Starting back in 2022 as Design Lead, managing today the platform as the Director.

01 - The results

## A structure only counts if it holds.

This one does - and the numbers are the system's, not mine.

It started with a bold call: switching a role to embrace a new direction, and selling it to my Head of Design to win the sponsorship it needed. From there - **zero users to a community of 2000+ practitioners**, and a system built to rest in many hands, never mine alone.

I set the direction, carried it to leadership, then handed my team the operational goal as OKRs of their own - always tied to the company's annual business objectives, so our ambition lived inside a shared project, never beside it.

Adopted, and depended on: when a team builds on Elements, 86% of their live interface is the system. The cost-rationalization bet, paid - teams build on it, not around it.

Like Beyfortus®. The wait between experts, removed. What used to take a handoff chain now ships in seconds - accessible, responsive, on-brand by default.

Adopted. Trusted. Extended.
**Everything a system needs to outlive the person who built it.**

**And none of it was measured after the fact.** I ran the platform on live instruments, read as decisions and not trophies: DORA metrics reviewed every sprint for delivery health, a practitioner satisfaction survey at least twice a year, product priorities re-reviewed quarterly against the business's own priorities, and end-of-session feedback from every community ritual.

The data didn't prove the work was good. It told me where to invest, what to deprecate, and which product needed support next - and it changed how we worked: we iterated our whole support model on it, reshaped the rituals, and introduced rotating roles because the signal told us to.

02 - The stakes

## One global and unified system.

Elements is Sanofi's unified experience system - **one design language** serving digital products and delivery platforms globally, from B2B and B2C platforms to internal tools, across products, brands and themes.

Some of those platforms carry hundreds of surfaces on their own - **one marketing platform alone owns the delivery of 200+ websites worldwide.** Systematize at the source, and you reach every one of them at once.

> Libraries

> Product Implementation

> Integrated support

> Global team

That's the scope that makes the stakes real.
**A system this size is two bets in one.**

**Cost rationalization** first, by mutualizing practice so every product team doesn't rebuild the same thing 45 times. Second, **experience at scale**: one coherent journey across every user touchpoint. Service design for an entire organization, from employee to patient.

Both bets only pay if the system is adopted. A system nobody trusts is the worst of both worlds: you fund the platform *and* the fragmentation it was meant to erase - while the experience stays broken touchpoint by touchpoint. And at this scale, every lost hour of velocity is lost across all of them at once.

Multi-brand · multi-theme · B2B / B2C / internal · from employee to patient · worldwide

03 - The problem

## Nobody meets.

A designer ships a spec. An engineer rebuilds it from scratch. A marketer briefs an agency for a palette the system already had. That's the tax. **Not incompetence - distance.** Three crafts, three silos, each solving in private what the others had already solved. And it wasn't one handoff, it was the whole ecosystem.

## The what was fragmented.

An ecosystem of products and touchpoints that didn't share a language, built on assets that didn't connect. Augmenting **design and engineering debts**, for every new product launch, at global scale.

## The how was worse.

Practices siloed team by team, none standardized. Everyone solving the same problems privately, in parallel, paying the same cost forty-five times over. This is where time goes. Not into the work - into the gaps between people redoing what already exists. Meanwhile the system itself can ship in seconds, digital constraints built in: accessible, responsive, on-brand by default. **The capability was there. The structure to use it wasn't.**

04 - The failure, and the decision

## You can't outsource the thing your whole system depends on.

That's the lesson. Here's the half I don't get to blame on anyone else.

The engineering was externalized before my time - not my call. But **the early bet I shared, and got wrong, was priority.** Sanofi's rebranding was a massive effort, and we poured the roadmap into brand alignment - a beautifully unified, on-brand system. Piloted on sanofi.com, we were boxed into its framework, and we quietly deprioritized what product teams actually needed.

[Deep dive in the origin story →](/system-brand)

So the system was coherent, and barely adopted. It took me too long to see it: we'd built it brand-first and at arm's length - for the brand, and through a vendor, but not for the people who had to build on it. **A design system nobody adopts is the oldest failure in the field** - and we'd walked straight into it.

So I stopped fixing the output and fixed both sources. I pushed to internalize engineering - inside Sanofi, one team, one objective: adoption. And I led the rebuild from zero - design foundations, Figma kit, React repos - this time from a holistic view of the whole portfolio and the experience it had to serve. Brand *and* product, connected, because I'd learned what it costs to choose one.

It wasn't the safe path. Internalizing means owning the budget, the hiring, the reorg - **a €1M bet on a model, not a line item.** I drove it, and I carried it through.

The best architecture I built started as the one I got backwards.

05 - What I owned

## The decision was the easy part.

**A decision is easy to claim - owning it isn't.** I didn't just call for internalization; I carried everything that came after.

I designed the target org - its capabilities, its roles, one operating model - and wrote the job descriptions that made it real. Then I hired into them: from one designer to the roles the system needed, and a Practitioner Experience capability I scoped, defined and staffed from nothing. The budget came with it, and the line between what the platform funded and what it shipped was mine to draw.

Governance was where I refused the easy answer. Two models, not one. A **hybrid, federated model** for how the system gets built - a centralized core my team owned, contribution opened outward through inner source, never fully federated: that's the trap. And a **tiered service model** for how support got prioritized, weighted by each product's place in the Sanofi portfolio and re-reviewed quarterly against the business's own priorities - so support followed strategy, not the loudest request.

**Scaling is a series of bets, and I refused to place them blind.** Every decision got the same reading: what it opened, what it exposed, what it cost to reverse. I surfaced them rather than waiting for them to surface - then locked each one with the team or my stakeholders, so no bet was mine alone to carry once it was made.

This is the layer no contributor can claim. **Not the components - the conditions.**

06 - The architecture

## You maintain a design system. You run a platform.

Elements isn't a single product anymore. **It's a platform** - several products under one governance, one pipeline, for one community. Three axes, six capabilities. And the tension is the point: they aren't parallel axes; each one only works because the other two hold their end.

Brand × Product
Corporate Experience

The Sanofi brand expressed across corporate and marketing surfaces: identity, theming, multi-brand coherence. **Where the brand becomes tangible.**

Brand × Product
Product Foundations

The mandatory building blocks of every experience: principles, accessibility, grids, spatial system, motion, data visualisation, theming. **The digital non-negotiables.**

System
Design System

The reusable, scalable foundation: token architecture, component libraries, integrated guidelines, engineering standards. **From token to production.**

System
Experience Patterns

Standardized but flexible design and interaction patterns: from internal AI-driven tooling to marketing interfaces, business-specific. **Learn once, apply everywhere.**

People
Practitioner Experience

The service layer around the people who use and contribute to the system: tiered service model, onboarding, unified tooling, rituals, academy. **The humans who use it, and those who craft it.**

People
Community of Practice

The governance and culture that turn contribution into practice: inner source model, contribution pathways, shared rituals, practice leadership. **The humans who grow it.**

### Seven products, one pipeline from design to dev.

- 01 Token architecture multi-brand, multi-theme, multi-surface

- 02 Libraries Figma and React, hosted on GitHub

- 03 Figma Companion palettes, accessibility checks, layout scaffolding directly from the canvas

- 04 Storybook source of truth, living, versioned documentation

- 05 Elements MCP integrated into AI design and engineering workflows

- 06 Integrated support channels Slack and Teams streams with automation layers

- 07 Elements Portal showcase website

**The pipeline is the thesis made physical.** Brand, design and engineering pour into the same chain - the wait between experts, engineered out. That's the ÷3: not people working faster, but expertises no longer waiting on each other.

### How it holds itself.

I didn't build a system I hold together. I built the conditions where it holds itself. **Autonomous streams**, split by capability, each owned and moving without a central decision - held inside a **collective framing**, so a stream deciding alone still decides inside a frame it co-wrote. **Shared rituals** are the connective tissue keeping five hubs one system rather than an archipelago. And every week, the same conversation: **is the work moving, and is the person growing.** A team that isn't well doesn't ship, so wellbeing isn't a nice-to-have on the side - it's load-bearing.

On top of that, **a standing feedback loop**: we didn't just ship the system, we questioned how we built it - interviewing practitioners, collecting feedback, testing our own ways of working, and reprioritizing support against what we heard. The system improves; so does the team that makes it.

And when a product team was under water, we got into the work *with* them - ego aside, hands in: reviewing their codebase, auditing with concrete proposals, co-building to share the load. That wasn't just me; it was the standard I set and asked everyone to carry. You don't point at the problem from across the room, you cross it. **A team that only points the way isn't leading; it's watching.**

I don't state values. I install them.

**Rigour — nothing ships on a hunch.**

No component, no roadmap line ever shipped without discovery: what the ecosystem actually needs, what already exists. Then one shared convention, written down - so the standard isn't a review at the end, it's the default at the start.

**Responsibility — the floor, not the reach.**

WCAG AA as the Sanofi digital standard, not our own bar. Full library audited to 100% before our first major, then held there - so no product team paid the cost downstream.

**Ethics — a constraint nobody asked me to keep.**

When AI entered the strategy, I set the line before we built: pragmatic where it accelerates the craft, absolute where it would decide for a human, quality guardrails non-negotiable throughout. Not everyone draws it. I do.

### The team that runs it.

**I owned the *why* and the *what*. They owned the *how*** - and made it sharper than I could have specified. Each capability runs itself, day to day, through the team I recruited and grew. Agile is the tempo, not a checkbox: the metronome keeping six capabilities and seven products in sync without a central hand. Nothing shipped without us aligned, and nothing waited on me to be decided.

For example, [Nicolas Chatelain](https://nicolaschatelain.com) was leading the People axis from Experience Operations enablement to Community of Practice engagement plan and POC. In parallel, [Rodolphe Peruzza](https://rodolphe.design/chat) was leading the System axis, from craft to experiences patterns industrialization.

Zero resignations
in three years

Design went from one to five. One contractor converted to full-time. Average completed engagement: **23 months against a one-year target.**

The people who left in 2026 left for reasons none of us chose - a group budget call, a product remap, a scoped engagement that ended as planned. **Not one left me.** That's not luck. People stay where their growth and their wellbeing are part of the plan, not an afterthought, the same care I built into the system, pointed at the team.

My job wasn't to hold it up.
**It was to build a structure that outlasts any of us.**

07 - The community

## A design system you install. A community you have to earn.

Most system owners treat community as a comms afterthought. I made it the strategy.

The bet: adoption never comes from mandate - only from ownership. So the system had to belong to its users, not to me. **A system nobody believes in dies with its founder; one a community owns, outlives them.**

**Experience runs both ways.** Elements serves end users - patients, healthcare professionals - but it also serves the people who build for them. A practitioner is a user too. So their practice gets the same care as any patient journey: fluid, productive, coherent across every perimeter. *Design the experience of the people designing the experience.*

consumer
→
contributor
→
maintainer

So I created the **Practitioner Experience capability** to make that real, and elevate one of my team members to run it, identifying how the career growth potential and the outcome for the company were seamlessly paired. I set the direction - community-led adoption, contribution over control, a path that graduates a practitioner from consumer to contributor to maintainer. My team proposed, built and ran the machine: the Elements Portal, the monthly Elements Demo, the office hours, the contribution and triage rituals, the onboarding path.

This is how **30+ designers across 5 international hubs** and **2000+ practitioners** became one community - end to end, from onboarding to production readiness. Not a mailing list: a practice, running on a contribution model adapted from Zalando's, where everyone gives at the level of their skill, from a flagged issue to a full component.

Taking a system from near-zero to 86% average usage ratio per product in production isn't a launch - it's **change management at scale**: shifting habits across more than 45 product teams, turning resistance into ownership one team, one hub, or even one priority at a time. It takes patience and it takes refusing - the two are the same discipline, not opposites.

And it's where careers grew, mine included: people levelled up by contributing and teaching, not by climbing a ladder - growth through the community, and reverse mentoring that kept me learning as much as I led.

08 - Where I'd take it next

## I removed the wait between design and engineering. There's one wait left.

I've argued Elements belongs closer to engineering - one reporting line from design to production. Not for tidiness: because **the border between design and engineering is dissolving.** AI-augmented workflows are already redrawing both crafts, and a structure that keeps them siloed slows everyone down.

Bringing them under one line isn't reorg for its own sake. It's **velocity**, and it's **proximity** - sitting end to end with product teams instead of handing work across expertise walls. I'd rather build that structure before the shift forces it than retrofit it after. The move I made once - agency to in-house - pointed here. **This is the next mile of the same road.**

Design systems ship standards.
Experience systems *grow culture.*
