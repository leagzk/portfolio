# Same systems thinking, just off the org chart.

Three weddings, three couples, three identities - this is the third: several days, **one identity carried from the first envelope to the last jar label**, and an escape game built from zero.

**No repository. No deploy pipeline. No org chart either** - nobody here reported to me, and nobody had to say yes. Just a creative pipeline, built end to end, and the proof that **a system is a way of working, not a technology.**

The living system

**Five words, three shapes and one palette**, pulled off two rented objects before anything was drawn. Short enough to brief from, complete enough that nothing had to be invented twice.

Not a mood. A short list you can hold someone to.

Paper, fabric, metal, wood. The signage nobody is meant to enjoy held the same line as the invitation.

9

suppliers, one guide

One page per zone, one owner per page. On the day it ran from the brief, not from me.

No client, no reviewer, and the only critic was the one who made it.

Nothing outside the work was going to say it was enough, or that it had drifted. And a line still had to be drawn between what I held and what belonged to people better at it than me.

A direction anyone could apply, and a production line that did not exist yet.

Two rented objects gave the language, then the foundations that carried it. The stationery would not compromise, so the artwork had to run on a press and cut on a machine at home - from one file.

Everything specified survived contact with the people who built it.

Drawn flat in Illustrator, briefed from a single guide, standing at the door twelve months later - and I spent the day inside the experience instead of behind it.

A game that mixed ninety strangers, and the same lever at work.

Six mechanics, six clues nobody could gather alone, and ninety people who arrived in blocks and left mixed. The same move behind a hackathon's 8,301 votes, a workshop run on a deck of cards, and an app that got application owners to keep their own records current.

## 01 &middot; The problem

Portability

A living system isn't one you finish. It's one that takes its real shape *in the hands of the people who use it.*

Here, off the screen, exactly as it does on it. Not product. Not digital. Not communication. A living one - and it held together just the same.

It's the proof I keep coming back to: **my way of working doesn't depend on the medium.** Systems thinking, brand coherence, experience design - they hold whether the output ships to production or lives in a garden for one day.

> The system, in the world

Client

Usually I do this to someone else's brief. This time *I was both ends of it.*

On the two editions before this one I worked to a couple's story: they had one, I translated it, and they told me when it was theirs. This time there was nobody on the other side of the table. **Brief, execution and review all sat on the same desk** - which quietly removes every check a brief normally comes with.

And I do this for a living: I like identities, I like things pixel-perfect. With no client review and no design crit, nothing outside the work was going to say it was enough, that it had drifted, or that a decision was a degree too loud for a day belonging to ninety people as much as to anyone.

**No client to please, and no client to stop me. The hardest arbitration on this project was against my own taste.**

Control

The line between what I hold *and what I hand over.*

On the first two editions I held all of it - art direction, print, setup, some of the hosting on the day. It worked, and it cost me the day itself. **You cannot both run an experience and be inside it.**

The other half of the same question is the suppliers. Nine of them, each better than me at their own craft. Hold too much and you get compliance instead of their best work; hold too little and fifty surfaces drift apart by the second week. The answer isn't a tighter brief, it's a clearer one - agreed at the start on what is fixed, what is theirs, how we work together, and what stays open for their own flexibility and invention. That last part isn't generosity: it's what lets everyone enjoy the thing from the inside instead of executing it from the outside.

And a direction still has to survive the room it's defended in. This one held three generations who don't value the same things - what reads as considered to one reads as cold to the next - and not one of them reports to anybody. Finding the argument that lands for each without moving the work itself is the job I do upstairs. **Stakeholder management, with family instead of a steering committee.**

**Fix the frame, open the rest.** It's the same arbitration I run on a design system: a core nobody negotiates, and everything around it contributed.

## 02 &middot; The approach

Direction

Starting from a constraint *you don't own.*

**The whole thing started with two objects.** Not a moodboard and not a colour study - two rented pieces we could actually put in the space. The **Popolo** plate: scalloped rim, hand-painted florals, cobalt and ochre. The **Roly Poly** armchair: fat legs, round shoulders, no straight line anywhere. One gave the pattern language. The other gave the posture. Then making every other decision agree with them - that's most of the job on the platform too.

Five words came out of them, written down before anything was drawn: **shapes, type, colors, conviviality and playfulness.** Then systematised end to end across the whole experience, from the first envelope to the last jar label.

The scallop became the die-cut on the stationery and the discs hung from the arch. The chair's soft geometry became the arched panels on the signage. The palette came straight off the glaze. And the last two - conviviality and playfulness, the ones that aren't visual properties at all - **are the reason the day ended up with an escape game in it.**

Those five words were the brief - mine, and everyone else's. **Every supplier was briefed from them, and every arbitration came back to them.** That is the whole of the creative direction: not a mood, a short list you can hold someone to.

Production pipeline

An art direction with *no production line behind it.*

The stationery is where the direction would compromise least: die-cuts taken off the plate's scalloped rim, exact papers, a suite that unfolds. No printer would run that at the volume and the price of a private event, and no machine I could put on my own table prints to that quality.

So I built the line rather than lower the bar. **One source file, two outputs** - artwork a professional printer could run, and the same artwork cut at home on a Cricut. Which meant a second cut-line system living inside the same document as the first, each one blind to the other's marks, neither allowed to pull the drawing out of true.

**A constraint, turned into an operating lever.** It is the problem a token set solves on the platform: one source of truth, several build targets, and not one of them permitted to degrade the others. Here the build targets were a press and a cutting mat.

## 03 &middot; The proof

Coverage

The test was *the boring surfaces.*

A monogram, a palette, signage, stationery, room tags, table names, favours, packaging - and the WhatsApp community that ran before any of it. **Dozens of touchpoints, one language**, the same coherence I hold across 45+ digital products, applied to paper, fabric, metal and wood.

Anyone can be charming on an invitation. The proof is on the signage nobody is meant to enjoy - where to leave your glass, how to find your room. Same display face, same stripes, same jokes.

That's where **conviviality and playfulness** stopped being words on a moodboard and became a rule I had to keep, on the two hundredth decision as much as the first.

Handover

### One page per zone, one owner per page.

Behind the identity and the staging: coordination. Florist, caterer, photographer, venue, rentals - a full ecosystem of suppliers, each with their own constraints, all pulling toward one coherent experience.

I wrote it all down. Zone by zone: what goes where, what it's made of, what it's for. **A production guide precise enough to be executed without me in the room** - then I handed the day itself to Hera & Harmonia, who ran it from the brief. **That's the whole method.**

Because the point was never to hold every thread myself. It was to build something clear enough that someone else could carry it, and to trust them to. On the day, the system held, and I got to be *in* the experience instead of running it.

The same move as everything else I build: design the system, iterate with stakeholders, brief the owners, step back and collect the feedback. It works in a repo. It works in a garden.

## Pivot &middot; Gamification

Mechanics

Tightly engineered underneath, *weightless on top.*

An escape game, designed from zero - rules, clues, progression, reward. A locked trunk, a sequence to crack, a payoff at the end. Experience design in its purest form: a system of constraints and steps that has to *feel* effortless while being tightly engineered underneath.

**Six riddles, six different mechanics, six clues** - geocaching across the grounds, a crossword, ciphers to break, puzzles to assemble - and one code at the end to open one trunk. Each had to be learnable in thirty seconds, solvable by people holding a drink, and unmistakably part of the same day as the invitation and the table.

> The game, set up in the courtyard

Participation

The brief was never a game. It was *ninety people who already knew their table.*

Every guest list arrives pre-sorted - families, school friends, the people from work - and left alone it stays that way all day. The room is perfectly pleasant and nobody meets anyone. **That was the actual brief**: a reason for people who don't know each other to need each other for twenty minutes.

So the mechanics were built for it rather than decorated with it. Six clues nobody could gather alone, held by people scattered across the grounds: the only route through was to go and ask a stranger what they had.

What I hadn't planned was the range. **Everyone played, the youngest to the oldest**, and the groups that formed had nothing to do with the ones that arrived. The mixing I designed for was the stated goal; what actually happened went past the design.

And it only became itself once people played it. I drew the frame - tight, deliberate, engineered underneath - and the experience took its real shape in their hands, in ways no plan could fully script.

Shipped empty. **Finished by the people who played it.**

Transfer

Gamification isn't a party trick. *It's how you get people to do the boring thing.*

The lever is the same one I reach for at work, and it has nothing to do with points and badges. It applies wherever a result depends on people choosing to take part, and no mandate can make them.

**Hackathons.** Sanofi's first ran on the brand platform, sponsored by the Chief Medical Officer and the Global Head of R&D: 6,000 participants, 103 ideas, 8,301 votes. The voting wasn't decoration around the event - it was the engine that made six thousand people show up for it.

**Ways of working.** Workshops built on a card game: situations to replay, in teams, instead of a deck about collaboration. A deck gets nodded at. A hand of cards gets argued with, and the argument is the point.

**Data quality.** I mentored a product designer on an app aimed at application owners and business stakeholders, whose job was to keep their own records current - the least rewarding task in any IT organisation. Turning it into something with progress, standing and visible completion is what produced a clean dataset of Sanofi's application landscape. **Nobody updates a database because they were asked twice.**

**Recognition.** An annual awards programme, back when I ran communication: the same mechanic at company scale, with status as the reward instead of a trunk.

An escape game, a hackathon, a design system: **you design them for the people who'll use them, not for yourself - and they come alive the moment you let go.**

The frame is mine. **The life is theirs.**

I don't repeat a system - I version it. Three editions, three couples, three identities, a different game every time. And nothing in this one was switched on for the occasion: a brief held to five words, a frame fixed with the rest left open, a handover precise enough to step out of - it is what I do at work, on a project nobody paid me for. **The drivers don't stop at the screen, and they don't stop at the org chart either.**
